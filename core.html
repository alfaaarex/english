What is a Factual Passage?

A factual (non-literary) passage presents information, facts, data, or descriptions — typically from newspapers, journals, science writing, government reports, or informational essays. The language is plain and precise. Questions test literal comprehension, inference, and vocabulary in context. Unlike discursive passages, factual passages do not present arguments — they inform.

Common topics: environment, health, technology, history, social issues, science, geography, economics.

Question Types and How to Attempt
  • Multiple Choice Questions (MCQs) — read the question first, then scan the passage for the relevant section. All options may be partially correct; choose the one best supported by the text.
  • Very Short Answer (VSA / 1 mark) — locate the answer directly. Write in a complete sentence, not just a word. Marks are given for accuracy.
  • Short Answer (2 marks) — answer in 2–3 sentences. Include two distinct points. Do not copy full sentences from the passage; paraphrase.
  • Vocabulary in Context — find the word in context, understand its use, then choose the synonym/antonym from options given. Never guess from the options alone.
  • Title / Heading — if asked, give a brief title (3–6 words) that captures the central idea, not just the topic. Avoid vague titles like "A passage about pollution."
Step-by-Step Strategy
  1. Read questions first (30 seconds) — know what you're looking for before you read the passage.
  2. Skim the passage — get the gist in one quick read. Identify topic, scope, and paragraph breakdown.
  3. Answer MCQs — go back to specific lines for each question. Underline key phrases in the margin.
  4. Write short answers in full sentences — even "1-mark" answers earn better marks when framed as sentences.
  5. Vocabulary last — use context (the sentence before and after) to determine meaning, not your general knowledge alone.
Examiner's Checklist — Common Mistakes
avoid
Copying lines verbatim. Examiners deduct marks if you simply reproduce a sentence from the passage as your "answer." Always rephrase.
avoid
Answering beyond the text. Never import your general knowledge into factual passage answers. Stick strictly to what the passage says.
do
Refer to specific paragraphs or lines when forming your answer. Precision signals careful reading.
do
Check word limits for short answers. Two-mark answers should be 20–40 words; no more. Padding with irrelevant material loses marks.
Sample Passage with Annotated Questions
Sample Passage — Factual ~420 words
India is home to over one-fifth of the world's population, yet it draws only about 4% of the world's freshwater resources annually. This dramatic imbalance is at the heart of the country's growing water crisis. According to a 2023 NITI Aayog report, nearly 600 million people in India face "high to extreme" water stress, and approximately 200,000 people die each year due to inadequate access to safe water. The causes are multiple and interlinked. Rapid urbanisation has placed enormous strain on existing water infrastructure. Cities like Bengaluru, Chennai, and Delhi regularly experience water shortages in summer months, forcing residents to rely on expensive private tankers. Meanwhile, agriculture — which accounts for about 80% of India's freshwater consumption — continues to use flood irrigation, one of the most wasteful methods possible. Subsidised electricity has historically encouraged farmers to pump groundwater freely, depleting aquifers far faster than they can be recharged. Climate change is compounding the problem. The monsoon, on which India is overwhelmingly dependent, has become more erratic. When it does rain, much of the water runs off as flood rather than percolating into the ground. Glaciers that feed India's northern rivers — the Ganga, Yamuna, and their tributaries — are retreating at an alarming rate, threatening long-term water supplies for hundreds of millions. Solutions exist. Drip irrigation can cut agricultural water use by 30–60%. Rainwater harvesting, once common across India, is being revived in states like Tamil Nadu, where rooftop harvesting is now legally mandatory in Chennai. Wastewater treatment and recycling, practised at scale in Israel and Singapore, remain underdeveloped in India. Policy reforms — ending electricity subsidies that incentivise over-pumping, pricing water more accurately, and legislating aquifer management — are politically difficult but essential. The crisis is not inevitable. India has addressed large-scale challenges before. What is required is the same combination of policy will, community participation, and technological investment that enabled the Green Revolution. The cost of inaction — social unrest, economic disruption, ecosystem collapse — is far higher than the cost of reform. Water security is not just an environmental issue. It is a matter of national survival.
Sample Questions with Model Answers
Q1 (MCQ) — What percentage of India's freshwater resources does the country draw annually?
  • (a) 20% (b) 10% (c) 4% (d) 1%
  • Answer: (c) — directly stated in the first paragraph. Strategy: locate "freshwater" in passage; answer is in line 2.
Q2 (2 marks) — What are the two main causes of India's water crisis mentioned in the passage?
  • Model Answer: The two main causes are rapid urbanisation, which strains existing water infrastructure, and wasteful agricultural irrigation practices, particularly flood irrigation which consumes 80% of India's freshwater. (29 words)
  • Examiner's note: Award 1 mark per correctly identified and briefly explained cause. Vague answers like "climate change and water use" earn partial credit only.
Q3 (2 marks) — Suggest two solutions mentioned in the passage to address the water crisis.
  • Model Answer: The passage recommends drip irrigation, which can reduce agricultural water use by 30–60%, and rainwater harvesting, which states like Tamil Nadu have already mandated for rooftops in Chennai. (31 words)
Q4 (Vocabulary, 1 mark) — Find a word in paragraph 3 that means "becoming worse / increasing in severity."
  • Answer: "compounding" — in "Climate change is compounding the problem." The context is key: climate change is adding to existing causes, making things worse.

What is a Discursive Passage?

A discursive passage presents a viewpoint, argument, or discussion on a topic — it analyses, persuades, or explores multiple sides of an issue. Unlike factual passages, they include opinion and reasoning. For this passage type, CBSE asks you to do note-making (organising the passage into a structured outline) and write a summary (condensing the passage in your own words).

Note-Making — Format and Rules

Notes are a structured outline of the passage. Think of it like a tree: main points are the trunk, sub-points are branches. You must use abbreviations (at least 3–4 of your own) and provide a key.

Note-Making Standard Format
Title: [Short, informative title for the whole passage] 1. [Main Point 1 — first major idea] 1.1 [Sub-point] 1.2 [Sub-point] 2. [Main Point 2] 2.1 [Sub-point] 2.2 [Sub-point] 2.2.1 [Sub-sub-point if needed] 3. [Main Point 3] 3.1 [Sub-point] Abbreviations Used: e.g. — e.g. (for example) | govt. — government | env. — environment | imp. — important
Note-Making — Rules and Marks Allocation
  • Title (1 mark) — must be short and informative. Avoid starting with "The importance of…" — just name the topic precisely.
  • Notes (3 marks) — 3 main headings with relevant sub-points. At least 2 sub-points per main heading. Must use abbreviations.
  • Abbreviations key (1 mark) — list all abbreviations you used with their full forms. Create your own; don't just use standard ones.
  • Notes must be in incomplete form — no full sentences. Drop articles (a, the), conjunctions, and verbs where possible.
  • Information must be accurate — do not invent points not in the passage.

Summary Writing — Rules

A summary is a condensed rewriting of the passage in your own words. It must be in continuous prose (not notes), in the third person, and within the word limit of 50–80 words (approx. one-third of the passage length).

  • Use your notes as a base — the notes already give you the main points; the summary is those points written as connected sentences.
  • No direct quotes from the passage — everything must be in your own words.
  • No new information — do not add anything beyond what is in the passage.
  • Logical flow — use connecting words: "furthermore," "however," "consequently," "as a result," "in addition."
  • Third person, past or present tense — avoid first-person pronouns.
Sample Summary ~75 words — full marks
India faces a severe water crisis, with a large population having access to only 4% of the world's freshwater. The crisis stems from rapid urbanisation, wasteful agricultural practices consuming 80% of available water, and the disruption of rainfall patterns due to climate change. Solutions such as drip irrigation, rainwater harvesting, and wastewater recycling are available, but sustained progress also demands policy reforms including the removal of electricity subsidies that encourage reckless groundwater extraction.
Summary — Common Mistakes
avoid
Exceeding word limit. Summaries over 80 words are penalised. Count your words; be ruthless with cutting adjectives and examples.
avoid
Using incomplete sentences. Summary is prose — not notes. Every sentence must be grammatically complete.
avoid
Including statistics or examples from the passage verbatim. Paraphrase "approximately 200,000 people die" as "a large number of deaths" or similar.
do
Cover all three main points identified in your notes. Missing a major section loses marks in both accuracy and completeness.

Purpose and Context

A notice is an official written announcement issued by an organisation, institution, or authority to inform a specific group of people about an event, decision, rule, or requirement. School notices are the most common type in CBSE exams — they are issued by a Head Boy/Girl, School Captain, or Secretary of a school body.

Notice — Standard Format
[NAME OF SCHOOL / ORGANISATION] NOTICE Date: DD Month YYYY [HEADING IN CAPITALS — brief, specific] Body text: What, Who, When, Where, Why. All essential details in concise sentences. No padding. [Name of issuing authority] [Designation]
Notice — The 5W Checklist (every notice must cover these)
  • What — What is the event/announcement?
  • Who — Who is this notice for? (Target audience)
  • When — Date and time of the event
  • Where — Venue / location
  • Why / How — Purpose or instruction (e.g., how to register, what to bring)
Sample Notice — Full Marks Answer
DELHI PUBLIC SCHOOL, ROHINI School name — do not leave blank NOTICE Word 'NOTICE' in caps, centred Date: 12 March 2025 Full date, not DD/MM/YY INTER-HOUSE ELOCUTION COMPETITION Heading in caps, specific All students of Classes IX to XII are hereby informed that the Annual Inter-House Elocution Competition will be held on 20 March 2025 (Wednesday) at 10:00 a.m. in the School Auditorium. The theme for this year is Technology: Blessing or Curse. Interested participants must register their names with their respective Class Teachers by 15 March 2025. 5W fully covered in <50 words Ritu Sharma Cultural Secretary Name + designation, not just a signature
Notice — Do's and Don'ts
do
Box the notice with a border — CBSE awards a format mark for this. Draw a neat rectangle around the entire notice.
do
Keep within 50 words. The notice body must be brief. Use no decorative language. Every word must be functional.
avoid
Never write "Dear Students" — a notice is not a letter. It begins directly with the body text after the heading.
avoid
No first person — never write "I am happy to inform you." Use third-person passive: "Students are informed that…"
avoid
Never use a salutation or closing. No "Yours faithfully" — notices are not letters.

Advertisement — Do's and Don'ts
do
Draw a box. A classified ad must be enclosed in a drawn border. This is worth a format mark.

do

avoid
Avoid first-person sentences like "I am selling my scooter." Write: "Honda Activa 5G, 2020 model, for sale."

About Posters

A poster is a visual communication tool designed to convey a message quickly and memorably. In CBSE exams, posters typically fall into two categories: awareness/campaign posters (about social issues like environment, health, road safety) and event posters (announcing a programme, competition, or exhibition). You cannot draw elaborate graphics in an exam, so the poster's impact comes from layout, headings, slogans, and concise text.

Poster — Standard Format (always in a box)
[ISSUING ORGANISATION — top] [Optional: small visual or symbol placeholder — describe it briefly] [MAIN HEADLINE — large, bold, 3–6 words] [2–3 key bullet points or short lines of information] [CATCHY SLOGAN — italic or underlined] [Date / Venue / Contact — bottom]
Sample Poster — Awareness (Save Water Campaign)
Issued by: Delhi Jal Board & Ministry of Water Resources [Image: A cracked, dry earth with a single water droplet] Brief description of visual — acceptable WATER IS LIFE. DON'T LET IT SLIP AWAY. ✦ Turn off the tap while brushing your teeth. ✦ Fix leaking pipes immediately. ✦ Harvest rainwater — every drop counts. ✦ Report water wastage: helpline 1800-XXX-XXXX "Save Water Today — Secure Tomorrow." Slogan — memorable, original World Water Day — 22 March 2025 | www.jalshakti.gov.in
Sample Poster — Event (Book Fair)
Kendriya Vidyalaya, Sector 8, Dwarka ANNUAL BOOK FAIR 2025 — Where Every Page Tells a Story — Dates: 18–22 March 2025 (10 a.m. – 5 p.m.) Venue: School Multipurpose Hall For: Students, Parents & Book Lovers ★ 200+ publishers ★ Up to 30% discount ★ Author Meets ★ Quiz and Poetry competitions for students "A reader today — a leader tomorrow." Entry Free | All are welcome
Poster — Key Tips
do
Include a slogan. The slogan is often worth 1 mark on its own. It must be original, concise, and memorable.
do
Use visual organisation: centre important text, use ★ ✦ ● as visual dividers in a written exam to suggest design hierarchy.
do
Describe your intended visual briefly in square brackets — examiners appreciate awareness of the visual medium even when drawing is not expected.
avoid
Don't write paragraphs. A poster uses bullets, short lines, and fragments — never prose paragraphs.

Purpose and Tone

A letter to the editor is written to a newspaper to highlight a public issue, voice concern, suggest a solution, or respond to a previous article. The tone is formal, civic-minded, and measured — never aggressive or slangy. You are a concerned citizen drawing public attention to a problem.

Letter to the Editor — Standard Format
Sender's Address House No. / Street City — Pin Code Date: DD Month YYYY The Editor [Name of Newspaper/Magazine] [City] Subject: [Concise subject line — 5–8 words] Sir / Madam, Para 1 — Introduce the issue, state your purpose. Para 2 — Describe the problem with details/evidence. Para 3 — Suggest solutions / call for action from authorities. Yours faithfully, [Your Full Name]
Sample — Letter to the Editor (Waterlogging in Colony)
12-B, Sector 14 Gurgaon — 122 001 15 March 2025 The Editor The Hindustan Times New Delhi Subject: Waterlogging in Sector 14 — An Urgent Appeal to Authorities Sir, Through the columns of your esteemed newspaper, I wish to draw the attention of the Municipal Corporation of Gurgaon to the severe waterlogging problem that has plagued Sector 14 for the past three monsoon seasons. The drainage system in our locality is completely choked, causing knee-deep water to stagnate on the main road and in residential lanes for days after every rainfall. The situation is not only a daily inconvenience for residents but also a serious health hazard — mosquito breeding and the risk of waterborne disease are significant concerns. Despite multiple complaints to the local ward office, no action has been taken. I urge the concerned authorities to inspect the drainage infrastructure immediately and undertake desilting before the onset of the 2025 monsoon. Public funds are collected through taxes; it is the duty of the administration to ensure basic civic amenities are functional. Yours faithfully, Priya Menon
Letter to Editor — Key Points
do
Opening phrase: "Through the columns of your esteemed newspaper…" — a conventional, mark-safe opening.
do
Three clear paragraphs: Problem → Details/Evidence → Solution/Request. Stick to this structure.
do
Close with "Yours faithfully" (not "Yours sincerely" — that is for personal letters where you know the recipient by name).
avoid
Never use aggressive or emotional language. "I am disgusted by the pathetic negligence" — too personal. Stay civic and measured.

Writing · Format 05
Letter of Complaint
6 Marks120–150 wordsFormal — Business Letter
Purpose

A complaint letter is written to a company, service provider, shopkeeper, or authority to report a deficiency, defective product, poor service, or unsatisfactory experience, and to seek a specific remedy. It is a business letter — the format differs slightly from a letter to the editor.

Complaint Letter — Standard Format
Sender's Address Street / Locality City — Pin Code Date: DD Month YYYY The Manager / The Customer Care Head [Company / Organisation Name] [Address] Subject: Complaint regarding [specific issue] Sir / Madam, Para 1 — State the purchase/service, date, order number if applicable. Para 2 — Describe the problem/defect specifically. Para 3 — State what you expect: refund, replacement, repair. Give a deadline. Yours faithfully, [Name]
Sample — Complaint about Defective Mobile Phone
45, Model Town Ludhiana — 141 002 18 March 2025 The Customer Care Manager SmartTech Electronics Pvt. Ltd. MG Road, Ludhiana Subject: Complaint Regarding Defective Mobile Phone (Order No. ST-2025-4421) Sir, I purchased a SmartTech Pro 12 mobile phone (Order No. ST-2025-4421) from your store on 28 February 2025, paying the full amount of ₹28,500. Within ten days of purchase, the device began exhibiting serious faults: the battery drains completely within two hours of a full charge, the rear camera fails to focus, and the phone restarts spontaneously. I visited your service centre twice but was informed each time that the problem could not be replicated — which is clearly incorrect. I request you to either replace the defective unit with a new, working device or issue a full refund within 10 days of receiving this letter, failing which I shall be compelled to approach the Consumer Forum for redressal. Yours faithfully, Harjeet Singh
Complaint Letter — Key Tips
do
Be specific: mention order numbers, dates, model names, amounts. Vague complaints like "the product was bad" lose content marks.
do
State a clear remedy and a deadline. The complaint must end with a specific request, not just a vague expression of dissatisfaction.
do
Mention consequences politely: "…failing which I shall be compelled to approach the Consumer Forum" — firm but professional.
avoid
Don't be rude or use threats. Aggressive language weakens your position and costs expression marks.

Writing · Format 06
Letter of Inquiry
6 Marks100–130 wordsFormal — Business/Official
Purpose

An inquiry letter seeks information about a course, product, service, event, or facility. You write to an institution, company, or organisation asking specific questions. The tone is polite and businesslike. Common contexts: enquiring about a coaching institute's courses, hostel facilities, a holiday package, or a product's specifications.

Inquiry Letter — Format (same as complaint letter; content differs)
Sender's Address + Date The Director / The Manager [of the institution you are enquiring about] [Organisation Name + Address] Subject: Enquiry regarding [course/product/service] Sir / Madam, Para 1 — Who you are, how you heard of the organisation, why you're writing. Para 2 — Specific questions (numbered is fine): fees, duration, eligibility, facilities, etc. Para 3 — Request for a brochure / reply / appointment. Thank them. Yours faithfully / Yours sincerely, Use "Yours sincerely" if you know the person's name; "Yours faithfully" if you don't.
Sample — Inquiry about Spoken English Course
34, Anand Vihar New Delhi — 110 092 20 March 2025 The Director Linguacraft Institute of Communication Connaught Place, New Delhi Subject: Enquiry Regarding Spoken English and Communication Skills Course Sir/Madam, I am a Class XII student appearing for my board examinations this year. I came across an advertisement for your Spoken English and Communication Skills course in The Times of India and would like to seek further information before enrolling. I would be grateful if you could clarify the following: 1. The duration and batch timings of the course; 2. Total fees and payment schedule; 3. Qualification and experience of faculty members; 4. Whether a beginner-level batch is available for students with limited prior exposure. I would appreciate it if you could send me a detailed brochure and prospectus at the above address at your earliest convenience. Yours faithfully, Sanya Kapoor

Structure of the Answer

This question requires you to write two things: (1) a covering letter applying for a post, and (2) a bio-data/résumé attached to that letter. Both must be written. The letter is concise; the bio-data is structured.

Application Letter Format
Sender's Address + Date The Hiring Manager / HR Head [Company Name + Address] Subject: Application for the post of [Job Title] Sir / Madam, Para 1 — Source of the job ad; name the post; express interest. Para 2 — Briefly highlight your qualifications, skills, and experience relevant to the post. Para 3 — State that bio-data is enclosed; request an interview; express confidence. Yours faithfully, [Name] Encl.: Bio-data
Bio-data / Résumé Format
BIO-DATA Name: [Full name] Father's Name: [Name] Date of Birth: [DD Month YYYY] Address: [Full address] Phone / Email: [Contact details] Academic Qualifications: Degree / Class — Board / University — Year — % Marks Work Experience: [If any; or write "Fresher" with internships] Skills: [Computer proficiency, languages, relevant skills] Hobbies/Interests: [Optional but adds character] References: [2 professional/academic referees — or "Available on request"] Declaration: I hereby declare that all the above information is correct to the best of my knowledge. Date: Signature: [Name]
Sample Application Letter
78, Vasant Kunj New Delhi — 110 070 22 March 2025 The Human Resource Manager TechBridge Solutions Pvt. Ltd. Nehru Place, New Delhi Subject: Application for the Post of Junior Content Writer Sir/Madam, With reference to your advertisement in The Hindu dated 18 March 2025, I wish to apply for the post of Junior Content Writer in your esteemed organisation. I have recently completed my Class XII examination from Delhi Public School, Vasant Kunj, with a score of 92% in English Core. I have a strong command of written English, experience in creative and academic writing, and proficiency in MS Office and basic content management tools. I am a regular contributor to my school's newsletter and won the All-Delhi Young Writers' Award in 2024. I am confident that my skills and enthusiasm make me a suitable candidate for the role. My bio-data is enclosed for your consideration. I would welcome the opportunity to attend an interview at your convenience. Yours faithfully, Ananya Joshi Encl.: Bio-data
Application + Bio-data — Tips
do
Write the Declaration in the bio-data. It is a standard part of the format and earns a mark.
do
Tailor the application letter to the specific job. Mention skills relevant to the post — don't write a generic "I am a hardworking student."
avoid
Don't repeat the bio-data in the letter. The letter highlights; the bio-data details. Avoid saying "As mentioned in my bio-data, I scored…" at length.
do
"Encl.: Bio-data" must appear below the signature of the letter. This is a standard professional convention.

Purpose and Style

An article is a piece of discursive writing for a school magazine, newspaper, or journal. It presents the writer's views on a topic in an organised, engaging way. Unlike a report (which is factual and objective), an article can include the writer's opinions, analysis, and recommendations. The style is readable, the tone is either formal or semi-formal depending on the topic, and the writing must have a clear thesis and logical development.

Article — Standard Format
[TITLE — Catchy, informative, 4–7 words] [Sub-heading — optional but adds marks] By [Your Name] Para 1 — Introduction: Hook + introduce the topic. (1–2 sentences of context; rhetorical question, striking fact, or vivid image to open.) Para 2 — Body Point 1: Problem / aspect 1 with evidence or example. Para 3 — Body Point 2: Another aspect, a contrasting view, or a deepening analysis. Para 4 — Solutions / Call to Action / Conclusion: End with a strong statement or recommendation.
Sample Article — Social Media and Mental Health
THE SILENT EPIDEMIC: SOCIAL MEDIA AND TEENAGE MENTAL HEALTH Is the scroll costing us our peace of mind? Sub-heading adds appeal By Aditya Sharma, Class XII-A We are the first generation to grow up entirely online — and perhaps the most anxious. With over 200 million Indian teenagers now active on social media platforms, the question of what this constant connectivity is doing to young minds is one that can no longer be deferred. Opening hook — statistics + question The problem is not social media itself, but the mechanics designed into it. Platforms are engineered for addiction — the endless scroll, the dopamine hit of a "like," the curated images of perfect lives that make ordinary existence feel inadequate. Studies consistently show a correlation between heavy social media use and increased rates of anxiety, depression, and low self-esteem among adolescents. Cyberbullying, once contained to school corridors, now follows children into their bedrooms. Yet blanket bans are neither realistic nor fair. Social media provides community for isolated teenagers, a platform for creativity, and access to information that previous generations could only dream of. The problem is not the tool but its unregulated, unconscious use. What we need is digital literacy — taught in schools, modelled by parents, and enforced by regulation. Platforms must be held accountable for algorithmically amplifying harmful content. And teenagers must be empowered to use technology with intention, not compulsion. Our wellbeing is not a side-effect of progress — it is the point of it. Conclusion: strong call to action
Article — Examiner's Marking Priorities
3 marks
Content: Is there a clear thesis? Are all aspects of the topic covered? Are ideas developed with examples or evidence?
2 marks
Expression: Is the language varied and appropriate? Are sentences well-constructed? Is vocabulary precise and interesting?
1 mark
Format: Is the title present? Is the author's name given? Is the article in paragraphs?
do
Use a strong opening. Begin with a fact, question, anecdote, or striking image — never with "In today's world…" (overused).
avoid
Avoid bullet points in an article. Articles are in prose paragraphs, not lists. Bullets are for reports and posters.

Speech — Standard Format

Sample Speech — The Importance of Reading Books
IN PRAISE OF BOOKS: WHY WE MUST READ IN THE AGE OF SCREENS Respected Principal, dear teachers, and my fellow students — a very good morning to you all. I, Meera Iyer, of Class XII-B, have the privilege today of speaking on a topic close to my heart: the irreplaceable value of reading books. We live in an age of ten-second videos and endless feeds. But have we stopped to ask: what are we actually learning? Books do something no reel can — they slow us down, force us to think, and transport us into lives we would never otherwise inhabit. A person who reads widely is, in a real sense, older than their years. They have lived a hundred lives before their own has properly begun. Reading also builds the skill that no exam can fully test but every career demands: the ability to think in structured, sustained, and nuanced ways. Our attention spans are shrinking; our tolerance for complexity is decreasing. Books are the antidote. My dear friends, I urge you: read one book this month. Not for your marks. Not for your teachers. Read it for yourself — and discover the person you might become. Thank you for your patient hearing. — Meera Iyer
Key Rhetorical Devices for Speeches (earn expression marks)
  • Rhetorical question: "Have we stopped to ask…?" — involves the audience, creates a reflective pause.
  • Anaphora (repetition): "Not for your marks. Not for your teachers. Read it for yourself." — rhythmic and powerful.
  • Direct address: "My dear friends…" — bridges the speaker-audience gap; makes the speech feel live.
  • Antithesis: "Before we lose what we never thought to value…" — contrast creates impact.
  • Emotive language: Appeals to pride, fear, hope — engages feelings, not just intellect.

Report Writing — Key Distinctions
do
Always write in the third person, past tense: "The event was held…" not "We held an event…"
do
For newspaper reports: lead paragraph is crucial. If an examiner reads only the first paragraph, they should know the entire story. Most important facts first.
do
Include one direct quote in a newspaper report (from an official or witness) — this earns a content mark and makes the report credible.
avoid
Never write opinion in a report. Reports are factual and objective. "The event was amazing" — wrong. "The event was attended by 500 students" — correct.

Debate — Standard Format
[TITLE — the motion] Greeting: Respected chair, honourable judges, teachers, and my dear fellow students… Opening: I, [Name], stand to speak in favour of / against the motion: "[exact motion as given]." Para 1 — State your position clearly. Give your first and strongest argument. Para 2 — Second argument with supporting evidence/example. Para 3 — Address and REFUTE the opposing side: "My opponents may argue… however, this ignores the fact that…" Conclusion: Restate position + appeal to audience: "I urge you all to vote in favour of / against this motion." Thank you.
Sample Debate — Against: "Examinations should be abolished"
MOTION: "EXAMINATIONS SHOULD BE ABOLISHED" (Speaking against the motion) Respected chair, honourable judges, teachers, and my dear friends — good morning to you all. I, Kavitha Rajan of Class XII, stand firmly against the motion that "Examinations should be abolished." The motion sounds appealing — who among us has not wished, mid-February, that exams would simply disappear? But to abolish examinations is to confuse the instrument of evaluation with the discomfort it causes, and that is a dangerous confusion. Examinations serve a vital function: they standardise evaluation across millions of students, ensuring that a certificate from any school in the country carries a common, verifiable measure of achievement. In a country as large and diverse as India, how else do we ensure fair access to higher education without a common benchmark? Remove the benchmark, and you remove one of the few genuinely meritocratic ladders available to students from disadvantaged backgrounds. The opponents of examinations cite stress and rote learning. I agree: these are real problems. But the solution is better examination design — not abolition. Replace recall-based testing with application, analysis, and skill-based assessment. Reform, not removal. Those who would abolish examinations would, in the name of reducing stress, dismantle the most powerful equaliser in our education system. I urge this house to vote against the motion. Thank you.
Debate — The Marks-Winning Formula
must
State your side clearly in the first sentence of the body: "I stand to speak in favour of / against the motion…" — missing this costs a format mark.
must
Refute the opposing view. A debate without refutation is an essay. Use: "My opponents may argue… but this ignores…" or "While it is true that… it is equally true that…"
do
Use rhetorical questions and direct appeals — they differentiate a debate from an article in tone and earn expression marks.
do
Close with a clear vote appeal: "I urge this house to vote in favour of / against the motion." — this is the standard, mark-safe closing for a debate.
avoid
Never present "both sides" as if you are undecided. You have been assigned a side. Equivocating ("on one hand… on the other…" without resolution) loses marks.
