Prose · Chapter 01
The Last Lesson
by Alphonse Daudet
Short StoryPatriotismLanguage & IdentityImperialism
About the Author

Alphonse Daudet (1840–1897) was a French novelist and short-story writer known for his vivid depictions of Provençal life and gentle irony. "The Last Lesson" is set in the aftermath of the Franco-Prussian War (1870–71), in which France ceded Alsace and Lorraine to Prussia. The story is a compressed meditation on loss, language, and what it means to belong to a culture when that culture is forcibly suppressed.

Plot Summary
Franz's Routine

Franz, a young student in Alsace, is late for school and dreading the grammar lesson he has not prepared. He considers skipping school entirely to enjoy the warm morning.

An Unusual Atmosphere

On the way he notices the blacksmith's apprentices reading a bulletin at the notice board. The school is uncharacteristically quiet. The villagers — including the ex-mayor, ex-postman, and other elders — are seated at the back of the classroom.

M. Hamel's Announcement

The teacher, M. Hamel, is dressed in his fine Sunday coat — the one he wore only on inspection days and prize-giving days. He announces that this is the last French lesson: Berlin has ordered that only German be taught in the schools of Alsace and Lorraine from now on.

Franz's Regret

Franz is overwhelmed with remorse. Everything he had found dull — his grammar, his books, his teacher — now seems precious. He finds he understands the lesson perfectly, as though he has never been so attentive.

M. Hamel's Final Address

M. Hamel speaks movingly about the French language — calling it the most beautiful, clearest, and most logical language in the world. He also blames the people of Alsace (and himself) for putting off learning. When the church clock strikes noon and the Prussian trumpets are heard, M. Hamel writes "Vive La France!" on the board, then dismisses the class with a gesture, too overcome to speak.

Characters
Franz
The narrator. A young, careless student who undergoes a sudden transformation of feeling. He discovers the value of his language and culture only at the moment of losing them — the story's central irony.
M. Hamel
The French teacher, devoted and dignified. In his final lesson he becomes a symbol of cultural resistance. His self-reproach is significant — he acknowledges his own past complacency.
The Villagers
Old Hauser, ex-mayor, ex-postman — they come to pay their respects to French as a dying institution. They represent collective guilt and collective mourning.
Themes
  • Language as identity. The loss of French is presented as the loss of self. M. Hamel calls French a "key" — those who keep their language hold the key to their prison. Language is not just communication; it is the vessel of memory, culture, and nationhood.
  • Imperialism and cultural suppression. The Prussian order to replace French with German is a classic colonial/imperial strategy: destroy the native language to erase cultural identity. The story was widely read as an allegory of French national feeling.
  • Regret and procrastination. Franz's sudden valuation of what he neglected is the human tendency to appreciate things only when they are lost. M. Hamel extends this to the entire community.
  • Patriotism. The quiet, dignified grief of teacher, student, and villagers is a form of patriotism — not martial, but deeply personal.
Literary Devices
  • Irony — Franz, who dreaded the lesson, suddenly finds himself understanding it. The very last lesson becomes the most meaningful.
  • Symbolism — M. Hamel's fine coat symbolises the solemnity of the occasion. The pigeons cooing outside symbolise France's spirit, which cannot be suppressed. The blackboard's final words "Vive La France!" are a symbolic act of resistance.
  • First-person narration — intimate and child-like, making the loss feel immediate and personal.
  • Imagery — the sunlight on the desks, the warm outdoor day Franz wanted to enjoy, contrasted with the weighted silence indoors.
  • Pathetic fallacy — the warm, sunny day outside contrasts cruelly with the cold finality inside the classroom.
Exam-Critical Facts
  • War referred to: Franco-Prussian War (1870–71).
  • Region: Alsace-Lorraine (now part of France; then ceded to Prussia).
  • M. Hamel's special attire: green coat, frilled shirt, embroidered black silk cap — worn on inspection/prize days.
  • M. Hamel called French: "the most beautiful language in the world — the most logical and the most clear."
  • Final words on the board: "Vive La France!"
  • Old Hauser's primer: he brings his old ABC primer and reads with the youngest children.
  • M. Hamel's self-blame: he admits he too put off teaching French and sent students to water his flowers.
  • The pigeons' cooing: a subtle symbol — even birds seem to mourn in French.
"My children, this is the last lesson I shall give you. The order has come from Berlin to teach only German in the schools of Alsace and Lorraine."— M. Hamel

Prose · Chapter 02
Lost Spring
by Anees Jung
Non-FictionPovertyChild LabourSocial Justice
About the Author

Anees Jung is an Indian journalist, author, and child-rights activist. The chapter is drawn from her book Lost Spring: Stories of Stolen Childhood (1997). It is divided into two sections: "Sometimes I find a rupee in the garbage" — about Saheb, a ragpicker in Delhi — and "I want to drive a car" — about Mukesh, a child from Firozabad's bangle-making community. Both sections are journalistic and compassionate, blending observation with quiet indignation.

Section 1 — Saheb-e-Alam
Who is Saheb?

Saheb-e-Alam (meaning lord of the universe — an irony) is a ragpicker of about 10–12 years, one of many Bangladeshi migrants who came to Delhi. His family left Dhaka when floods swallowed their home. They drifted to Delhi's streets.

His Dream

Saheb wanders the garbage heaps of affluent south Delhi each morning. He dreams of going to school. When the author jokingly promises to open a school "someday," Saheb turns up eagerly at her gate every morning.

The Teahouse Job

Saheb eventually finds a job at a local tea stall. He earns 800 rupees a month and all his meals. Yet the author observes that the "carefree look" has gone. He is no longer his own master — he carries a steel canister for his employer and has lost the only freedom he had: of roaming at will.

Section 2 — Mukesh
Firozabad

Firozabad is famous for its bangle industry. Thousands of children work in glass furnaces in darkness, risking blindness from the glass dust and fumes. Generations are trapped in this trade by poverty and the stranglehold of middlemen, moneylenders, and bureaucrats.

Mukesh's Dream

Mukesh is a boy from a bangle-making family. Unlike most children there, he dares to dream of becoming a motor mechanic. He wants to drive a car. His is an act of individual courage against collective fatalism.

The Trap

The author calls Firozabad a web: the debt, the sahukars (moneylenders), the middlemen, the police, the politicians — all conspire to keep the children in the furnaces. Any attempt to form a cooperative is crushed. The children have never known anything else, so they don't know what they are missing.

Characters
Saheb-e-Alam
A Bangladeshi migrant ragpicker. His name, "lord of the universe," ironically contrasts with his reality. His freedom — even while scavenging — is lost when he takes a "real" job.
Mukesh
A boy from Firozabad's bangle-making community who dares to dream beyond his inherited fate. His dream of being a motor mechanic symbolises individual agency against systemic oppression.
Mukesh's Father / Grandfather
Representative of generations trapped in the same trade. The father's eyes are filled with "cataracts" — both literally from the glasswork, and metaphorically, from hopelessness.
Themes
  • Poverty as a cycle. Neither Saheb nor Mukesh can break free easily. Structural forces — debt, caste, corruption — perpetuate poverty across generations.
  • Irony of names and titles. "Saheb-e-Alam" means lord of the universe; "Lost Spring" echoes lost childhood. These ironies are deliberate and cutting.
  • Loss of childhood. "Spring" metaphorically refers to youth, and it is stolen by labour. The "garbage" these children sift through is also the garbage of a society that fails them.
  • Freedom vs. security. Saheb's job provides food and money but strips his freedom. This is a comment on wage labour — even legitimate work can enslave.
  • The power of dreams. Mukesh's dream is fragile but defiant. It is the only thing the system cannot take from him. Whether he achieves it or not, the dream itself is an act of resistance.
Exam-Critical Facts
  • Saheb's full name: Saheb-e-Alam — meaning "lord of the universe."
  • Saheb's origin: Bangladesh (Dhaka); his family fled after floods destroyed their fields.
  • Saheb's wage at tea stall: 800 rupees/month + meals.
  • City of bangles: Firozabad, Uttar Pradesh.
  • Mukesh's dream: to become a motor mechanic and drive a car.
  • The "web" trapping children: sahukars, middlemen, policemen, keepers of law, politicians.
  • Hazard of bangle-making: blindness from furnace glare and glass dust.
  • Author's book: Lost Spring: Stories of Stolen Childhood.
"Saheb is no longer his own master."— Anees Jung, on Saheb taking the tea-stall job
"I want to drive a car."— Mukesh, his defiant dream
Likely Exam Questions
Q1What is ironic about the name "Saheb-e-Alam"? How does his life reflect this irony?
Q2Why does the author say Saheb is "no longer his own master" after taking the tea-stall job?
Q3Describe the "web" that traps the bangle-makers of Firozabad.
Q4What does Mukesh's dream signify in the context of systemic poverty?
Q5How does Anees Jung use the title "Lost Spring" symbolically?
Q6Compare the situations of Saheb and Mukesh as victims of poverty.

Prose · Chapter 03
Deep Water
by William O. Douglas
AutobiographyFear & CourageSelf-Conquest
About the Author

William Orville Douglas (1898–1980) was an Associate Justice of the United States Supreme Court — the longest-serving Justice in history (36 years). He was also an avid outdoorsman and author. This autobiographical essay is excerpted from his memoir Of Men and Mountains (1950). It narrates his childhood trauma of near-drowning and his determined adult effort to conquer his phobia through professional instruction.

Plot Summary
The YMCA Pool Incident (Age 10–11)

A big boy at the Yakima YMCA pool dunks Douglas — a non-swimmer — into the 9-foot deep end as a prank. Douglas sinks, tries to jump off the bottom, fails repeatedly, and nearly drowns. His lungs fill with water. He falls into unconsciousness with a green, frightening sensation. He survives but is left with a deep terror of water.

Childhood Fear Confirmed

Years earlier, as a 3–4 year old, his father had taken him to the California beach. Waves knocked him down and swept over him. The feeling of suffocation left a residual fear that the YMCA incident reactivated and entrenched.

Impact of the Phobia

The terror keeps him from canoeing, fishing, swimming — activities he loves. He tries to overcome it rationally but fails. He decides to hire a swimming instructor.

Professional Training

His instructor begins with a series — first on a rope and pulley, then swimming in segments: legs, breathing, full stroke. Each element is practised for weeks. By October he can swim a hundred yards.

Residual Terror — And Its Defeat

Even after formal training, traces of fear persist in deep water. He swims Lake Wentworth in New Hampshire, and Warm Lake, and each time confronts and defeats the residual panic. Finally, standing in the water, he pronounces himself free. He reflects on what he has gained: the fullness of life that comes from conquering fear rather than running from it.

Themes
  • Conquering fear through determination. Douglas's story is not about natural bravery but about deliberate, methodical effort. Courage is not the absence of fear — it is the decision to face it.
  • The fullness of life vs. its terror. The essay ends with a reflection: only those who have known death (or near-death) can know the full sweetness of life. Fear withheld Douglas from the life he wanted; conquering it expanded his world.
  • Mind over body. The training is partly physical but fundamentally psychological. It is the mind's terror — not the body's inability — that had paralysed Douglas.
Literary Devices
  • Detailed sensory imagery — the green, suffocating sensation underwater; the paralysis of limbs; the roaring in the ears. Douglas renders near-drowning viscerally.
  • Metaphor — water as a symbol of fear, death, and ultimately renewal. Douglas "defeats" the element that once almost destroyed him.
  • Suspense and pacing — the YMCA incident is narrated slowly, almost in slow motion, to convey the distorted time-sense of panic.
  • First-person introspection — the essay blends action with psychological analysis, making it both a narrative and a philosophical meditation.
Exam-Critical Facts
  • Douglas's age at YMCA incident: ten or eleven.
  • YMCA pool depth: 9 feet (deep end).
  • The big boy: his identity is never given — he tossed Douglas in "just for fun."
  • First fear of water: California beach (age 3–4), when waves knocked him down.
  • Douglas's book: Of Men and Mountains (1950).
  • Place of final conquest: Lake Wentworth, New Hampshire and Warm Lake.
  • Training technique: rope and pulley system, then segment practice over several months.
  • Quote from Roosevelt that Douglas echoes: "All we have to fear is fear itself."
"I was now the master of water."— William O. Douglas, after overcoming his phobia
Likely Exam Questions
Q1Describe Douglas's experience at the YMCA pool. How did it affect his life?
Q2How did Douglas's swimming instructor help him overcome his fear step by step?
Q3"All we have to fear is fear itself." How does this statement apply to Douglas's experience?
Q4What does the story teach us about overcoming phobias? What is the role of willpower?
Q5How does Douglas describe his sensation of drowning? Why is the description so detailed?

Prose · Chapter 04
The Rattrap
by Selma Lagerlöf
Short StoryRedemptionHuman GoodnessAllegory
About the Author

Selma Lagerlöf (1858–1940) was a Swedish author and the first woman to win the Nobel Prize in Literature (1909). She is celebrated for her mythological storytelling rooted in Swedish landscape and folklore. "The Rattrap" blends a realistic social portrait of a vagabond with a philosophical parable about the capacity of human kindness to redeem even the most degraded soul.

Plot Summary
The Peddler

An unnamed vagabond sells small rattraps made from wire scraps. He survives by begging. He reflects on life as a rattrap: the world sets baits — riches, joys, food — and men are caught in it as rats are.

The Crofter's Hospitality

The peddler is welcomed by a lonely old crofter (tenant farmer) who offers him porridge, tobacco, and warmth. The crofter shows him 30 kronor hidden in a leather pouch — his payment for selling cream. The next morning the peddler steals the money and leaves.

Lost in the Forest

The peddler enters the forest to avoid the main road and becomes completely lost. He walks in circles. He feels he has fallen into a rattrap himself — caught by his own greed. In a state of despair and exhaustion he stumbles upon an iron foundry.

Ironmaster's Mistake

The ironmaster, Edla Willmansson's father, mistakes the peddler in the dim light for an old regimental comrade named Nils Olof. He insists on bringing him home for Christmas. The peddler, cold and hungry, agrees — but is terrified of being discovered.

Edla's Kindness

Edla Willmansson, the ironmaster's daughter, shows the peddler genuine, unconditional hospitality — a Christmas gift and a letter asking him to return the following year "as welcome as a valued guest." Even after he is exposed as a thief and a vagabond by the ironmaster, Edla insists he be allowed to stay for Christmas.

Redemption

The next morning, Edla finds a small rattrap left as a Christmas gift. Inside are the stolen 30 kronor and a note from "Captain von Stahle" — the peddler's assumed identity — asking her to return the money to the crofter. He signs off as Captain von Stahle, adopting the dignified identity she had offered him. He has been redeemed by her trust.

Characters
The Peddler
Nameless vagrant. Cynical philosopher of the rattrap. Steals, lies — yet retains a moral core that Edla's kindness activates. He transforms from a rat caught in his own trap to "Captain von Stahle."
Edla Willmansson
The ironmaster's daughter. The story's moral anchor. Her empathy is unconditional — she treats the stranger with dignity regardless of his social position. Her kindness is the catalyst for his transformation.
The Ironmaster
Well-meaning but driven by social assumptions. He offers hospitality only because he mistakes the peddler for a former officer. When the mistake is clear, he wants to throw the man out — only Edla's plea saves the evening.
The Crofter
Lonely, generous, trusting — and robbed. His trust is exploited. He represents the vulnerability of simple goodness.
Themes
  • The rattrap as central metaphor. The world is a rattrap — baited with wealth, food, warmth. Greed traps people. The peddler falls into his own trap the moment he steals. The only escape is through virtue, not cunning.
  • Redemption through human kindness. What laws, poverty, and hardship could not achieve — restoring the peddler's humanity — is accomplished by Edla's unconditional trust. This is the story's central moral proposition.
  • The conditional vs. unconditional good deed. The ironmaster's generosity is conditional (he thinks the man is someone else). Edla's is unconditional. Only unconditional kindness transforms.
  • Identity and dignity. The peddler signs his final note "Captain von Stahle" — claiming the dignity Edla offered him. The name is not a lie but an aspiration she made real.
Exam-Critical Facts
  • Stolen amount: 30 kronor (Swedish currency).
  • The crofter's income: payment for selling cream from his cow.
  • Mistaken identity: peddler mistaken for Nils Olof, old regimental comrade.
  • The ironmaster's profession: ironmaster at Ramsjo Ironworks.
  • Edla's Christmas gift to peddler: a suit of clothes.
  • Peddler's alias in his final note: Captain von Stahle.
  • Author: Selma Lagerlöf — first woman Nobel laureate in Literature, 1909.
  • Setting: Sweden, around Christmas.
"Please let him stay. He may be a thief or a vagabond, but he is a human being, and Christmas is a time of joy and kindness."— Edla Willmansson
Likely Exam Questions
Q1Explain the rattrap as a central metaphor in the story. How does the peddler fall into his own trap?
Q2Compare the hospitality shown by the crofter, the ironmaster, and Edla. Whose kindness is most significant and why?
Q3What does the peddler's final note as "Captain von Stahle" signify about his transformation?
Q4How does Edla Willmansson bring out the best in the peddler? What qualities does she represent?
Q5The world is a rattrap — discuss how this philosophy plays out through different characters in the story.

Prose · Chapter 05
Indigo
by Louis Fischer
BiographyCivil DisobedienceGandhiColonialism
About the Author

Louis Fischer (1896–1970) was an American journalist and Gandhi's biographer. This chapter is excerpted from his biography The Life of Mahatma Gandhi (1950). It narrates the Champaran Satyagraha of 1917 — Gandhi's first major civil disobedience campaign in India. It deals with the exploitation of indigo farmers in the Champaran district of Bihar by British planters, and Gandhi's successful campaign for justice.

Background — The Indigo System

Under the tinkathia system, peasants were compelled to cultivate indigo on 3/20ths (approximately 15%) of their land and surrender the entire crop as rent to British planters. When synthetic dye replaced natural indigo in the 20th century, the British wanted to cancel this arrangement — but only after making the peasants sign agreements indemnifying them. Many peasants had already paid; they wanted their money back.

Plot Summary
Rajkumar Shukla's Invitation

At the 1916 Lucknow Congress session, Rajkumar Shukla — an illiterate, tenacious peasant from Champaran — follows Gandhi for weeks, pleading with him to visit Bihar. Gandhi eventually agrees.

Arrival in Champaran

Gandhi arrives in Champaran and is immediately served a notice to leave by the British authorities. He refuses, citing his duty to the people. He is summoned to court but the case is dropped — the first defeat of civil authority before satyagraha.

Investigation

Gandhi interviews thousands of peasants to document the injustice. He is assisted by lawyers from several provinces including J.B. Kripalani and Mahadev Desai. The planters have no answer to Gandhi's systematic documentation.

The Enquiry Committee

The Lieutenant-Governor sets up an official enquiry commission and includes Gandhi. The commission recommends a 25% refund to the peasants. Gandhi accepts it — not because the amount is adequate, but because it breaks the planters' prestige. Within a few years, the planters abandon the estates entirely and the tinkathia system ends.

Social Work

Gandhi sets up primary schools, medical clinics, and promotes clean habits in the villages. He enlists volunteers from across India, including his wife Kasturba. He believes political freedom must be accompanied by social upliftment.

Themes
  • Satyagraha — non-violent resistance. Gandhi's defiance of the British order to leave, without aggression, is the founding moment of civil disobedience in Indian nationalist politics. This episode became a template for the independence movement.
  • Self-reliance. Gandhi refuses help from the local Congress organisation, insisting the movement must grow from within Champaran. He also tells lawyers they should be prepared to go to jail themselves — civil disobedience requires personal sacrifice.
  • Dignity over quantity. Accepting 25% rather than 100% was a moral and strategic decision: the admission of wrongdoing mattered more than the money.
  • Colonialism and exploitation. The tinkathia system is a clear example of colonial economic exploitation — forced cultivation, loss of choice, and systematic impoverishment.
Exam-Critical Facts
  • Year of Champaran Satyagraha: 1917.
  • Rajkumar Shukla's description: illiterate but resolute peasant.
  • Gandhi first met Shukla at: 1916 Lucknow Congress session.
  • Tinkathia system: cultivation of indigo on 3/20ths of land.
  • Refund amount accepted: 25% (half the 50% demanded).
  • Key associates: J.B. Kripalani, Mahadev Desai, Kasturba Gandhi.
  • Fischer's biography: The Life of Mahatma Gandhi.
  • Gandhi's lesson to lawyers: be prepared to go to jail — civil disobedience cannot be outsourced.
"The battle of Champaran is won."— Gandhi, after the Government dropped the case
Likely Exam Questions
Q1What was the tinkathia system? How did it exploit the peasants of Champaran?
Q2Why did Gandhi accept a 25% refund instead of demanding 100%? What was his reasoning?
Q3How does the Champaran episode demonstrate the power of satyagraha?
Q4What was the role of Rajkumar Shukla in bringing Gandhi to Champaran?
Q5What social reforms did Gandhi undertake alongside the political campaign in Champaran?
Q6What lesson did Gandhi teach the lawyers who came to assist him in Champaran?

Prose · Chapter 06
Poets and Pancakes
by Asokamitran
Humorous EssayFilm IndustryIronyAspiration
About the Author

Asokamitran (1931–2017) was a Tamil writer who worked for nearly three decades as a stenographer and clerk at Gemini Studios, one of India's most famous early film production companies in Chennai (Madras). His memoir My Years with Boss and associated essays offer a ground-level, wry view of the studio's glamour and its mundane realities. "Poets and Pancakes" is a collection of comic vignettes drawn from his Gemini days.

Key Vignettes
The Makeup Department

Gemini Studios had a large, well-supplied makeup department. Pancake makeup (Pancake was the brand name) was applied liberally to transform actors — often hilariously, since the cameras did not always notice. The brand name becomes the title's comic pivot.

The Office Boy's Grievance

A young office boy — with literary ambitions — resents being assigned only to bring tea, run errands, and be perpetually ignored. He believes he is wasted talent and blames Subbu (S. D. Burman's associate) for his obscurity. His complaint is a comic study in misplaced envy.

Subbu — The Story Department

Kothamangalam Subbu (S. S. Vasan's right-hand man) is celebrated as a genius fixer — a man who could solve any script problem, please the boss in any mood, and write Tamil verse as easily as breathing. Yet his genius breeds resentment. The author describes him with admiring irony: "No one can think of Gemini Studios without thinking of Subbu."

The Legal Adviser

A lawyer who joined as a legal adviser finds himself acting in films — against his will, playing comedic villain roles. He is embittered, feeling his legal talents are wasted. Another ironic portrait of misaligned ambition and utility.

The English Poet's Visit

The Moral Rearmament Army (MRA), a international movement, visits Gemini Studios with a play. Later an English poet — whose name and work remain mysterious to Asokamitran — visits and delivers a speech. The author and his colleagues could not understand him. It is only much later that the author discovers through a magazine that the visitor was Frank Buchman's MRA and the poet was likely Stephen Spender or one of the famous poets of that era. The irony: a world-famous poet had visited, and no one at the studio understood a word he said.

Characters
Subbu (Kothamangalam Subbu)
Gemini's genius — multitalented, loyal, a prolific writer of scripts and Tamil verse. Represents how talent becomes threatening to those around it. He is the "boss's man" but also a genuinely gifted artist.
The Office Boy
An unnamed, embittered young man who believes his literary talent is unrecognised. His resentment of Subbu is comic — and poignant — since his actual talent is never demonstrated.
The Legal Adviser
A lawyer turned unwilling actor. Represents those whom circumstance has miscast. His bitterness is understandable; his role is comedic.
S.S. Vasan ("The Boss")
Proprietor of Gemini Studios. A powerful, inscrutable figure whose whims ruled the studio. Never fully characterised — known only through his effect on others.
Themes
  • Irony and misplaced ambition. Each character believes they are wasted — the office boy, the lawyer, even Asokamitran himself. The essay gently mocks the gap between self-perception and reality.
  • Art in a commercial environment. Gemini Studios is a dream factory, but it is also a workplace with hierarchies, jealousies, and absurdities. Art is produced in deeply unromantic conditions.
  • The insignificance of individuals in large systems. No individual at the studio truly matters — not the makeup artists, not the office boy, not even the visiting English poet who nobody could identify or understand.
Exam-Critical Facts
  • Studio: Gemini Studios, Madras (Chennai).
  • Proprietor: S.S. Vasan.
  • Subbu's full name: Kothamangalam Subbu.
  • "Pancake": brand name of theatrical makeup, not a food item.
  • Visiting group: Moral Rearmament Army (MRA).
  • The unidentified English poet: possibly Stephen Spender (later discovered through an article).
  • Asokamitran's role at Gemini: stenographer/clerk.
  • Subbu's special talent: he could produce Tamil verse and solutions to script problems with equal ease.
"No one can think of Gemini Studios without thinking of Subbu."— Asokamitran
Likely Exam Questions
Q1Who was Subbu and why was he envied at Gemini Studios?
Q2What is the significance of the title "Poets and Pancakes"?
Q3Why is the office boy bitter? What does his character represent?
Q4What was the moral of the English poet's visit to Gemini Studios?
Q5How does Asokamitran use irony and humour to portray life at Gemini Studios?

Prose · Chapter 07
The Interview
Christopher Silvester (Part I) & Umberto Eco (Part II)
Non-FictionMediaCelebrity & PrivacyInterview as Art Form
About the Chapter

This chapter has two parts. Part I is an extract from Christopher Silvester's introduction to The Penguin Book of Interviews (1993), which provides a brief history of the interview as a journalistic and literary form. Part II is an extract from a 1995 interview with Umberto Eco — Italian novelist, philosopher, and semiotician — conducted by Mukund Padmanabhan for The Hindu. Eco discusses how his academic writing and his famous novels coexist, and how he uses the "interstices of busy-ness."

Part I — A History of the Interview
Origin

The interview as a journalistic form emerged in the mid-19th century. It became widespread in the 1860s, especially in the United States.

Reactions

Silvester notes that great figures have viewed interviews with hostility: Rudyard Kipling called the interview "an assault." Lewis Carroll refused to be interviewed. H.G. Wells called it "the most tremendous bore." Others saw it as an intrusion into privacy.

The Interview's Power

Yet interviews have also become a democratic art form — they democratise access to the famous, bring out aspects of personality that no biography can, and shape public opinion. Silvester argues the interview has become the primary mode of mass communication.

Part II — Umberto Eco
Academic vs. Novelist

Eco is a scholar (his academic work includes semiotics, medieval aesthetics, linguistics) who also wrote bestselling novels (The Name of the Rose, Foucault's Pendulum). Critics were puzzled that a serious academic wrote popular fiction. Eco dismisses the contradiction — both are ways of asking the same questions.

The Interstices

Eco explains that he writes in the "interstices" — the small spaces between other activities: waiting for someone, riding the metro. He has a horror of wasted time and trains himself to use every gap.

The Name of the Rose

Eco says he wrote the novel at 50 because he felt like writing a novel. He began with an image of a monk being poisoned. It became a medieval mystery that also functions as a philosophical inquiry into interpretation, truth, and the power of books.

Themes
  • The interview as power and intrusion. The chapter explores the tension between public interest and private life that the interview embodies. It can be invasive, reductive, or distorting — yet also illuminating.
  • Blurring of high and popular culture. Eco's career challenges the hierarchy between "serious" academic work and "popular" fiction. He treats both with the same intellectual rigour.
  • Productivity and time. Eco's philosophy of the "interstices" is a meditation on creative discipline — how great productivity comes not from huge blocks of free time but from disciplined use of small pockets.
Exam-Critical Facts
  • Silvester's book: The Penguin Book of Interviews (1993).
  • Rudyard Kipling on interviews: "an assault".
  • Lewis Carroll: refused all interviews.
  • H.G. Wells: called interviews "the most tremendous bore."
  • Eco's most famous novel: The Name of the Rose.
  • Eco's concept: "interstices of busy-ness" — gaps between activities used for writing.
  • Eco began writing the novel at: age 50.
  • Interviewer of Eco: Mukund Padmanabhan for The Hindu.
  • Eco's academic field: semiotics, medieval philosophy, linguistics.
"I belong to a generation that believes in work, even when working means playing."— Umberto Eco

Going Places
by A.R. Barton
Short StoryFantasy & RealityAdolescenceHero Worship
About the Author

A.R. Barton is a contemporary writer whose story "Going Places" explores the inner world of adolescence — its fantasies, hero-worship, and the painful boundary between dream and reality. The story is set in a working-class neighbourhood and follows Sophie, a teenage girl, through a few days of private fantasy and its quiet collapse.

Plot Summary
Sophie's Dreams

Sophie, a student from a modest working-class family, fantasises about becoming a boutique owner, actress, or fashion designer. She tells her friend Jansie — both are likely to end up in a biscuit factory — about her grand plans. Jansie is realistic; Sophie is not.

The Hero

Sophie is infatuated with Danny Casey, a young Irish football player who is a local hero. She tells her brother Geoff that she met Danny Casey and that he promised to meet her by the canal the following week. Geoff is sceptical but secretly believes her.

The Fantasy Meeting

Sophie waits by the canal at the appointed time. Danny Casey does not come. It becomes clear to the reader that the "meeting" she told Geoff about was a fantasy — perhaps she saw him briefly, or perhaps she invented it entirely. In her imagination, the meeting had been warm and intimate.

Disappointment and Reality

Geoff had told their father about Sophie's claim, and when Casey does not appear, the family is implicitly embarrassed. Sophie walks home in the dark, alone with her feelings. The story ends quietly — her dream is not dramatically crushed but simply unrealised.

Characters
Sophie
The protagonist. A dreamer from a working-class family who uses fantasy as escape from limited prospects. Her hero-worship of Danny Casey is partly about freedom, stardom, and a life larger than her own.
Geoff
Sophie's older brother. Quiet, mechanical (he is an apprentice mechanic). He is Sophie's main confidant. He travels widely in imagination if not in reality — Sophie envies his freedom.
Jansie
Sophie's practical, grounded friend. She gently cautions Sophie that their futures are likely to be modest. She is a foil to Sophie's romanticism.
Danny Casey
A young Irish football star. He exists mostly as a projection of Sophie's imagination. Whether he actually met her or not is deliberately left ambiguous.
Themes
  • Fantasy vs. reality / adolescent escapism. Sophie's imagination is a refuge from the claustrophobia of her actual life — cramped house, limited options, predictable future. Fantasy is necessary but also dangerous.
  • Hero worship. Danny Casey is the object of Sophie's projection of all the freedom, glamour, and possibility she lacks. Hero worship is a form of displaced self-longing.
  • Class and limited aspiration. The story subtly critiques how class constrains imagination. Jansie's realism is shaped by acceptance; Sophie's fantasy is a silent protest.
  • Isolation. Despite having family, Sophie is emotionally alone — her father doesn't listen, her mother is absent, Geoff is emotionally unavailable. Fantasy fills the void.
Exam-Critical Facts
  • Sophie's aspirations: boutique owner, actress, fashion designer.
  • Likely reality for Sophie and Jansie: biscuit factory.
  • Danny Casey's nationality: Irish.
  • Danny Casey's profession: young football player.
  • Geoff's job: apprentice mechanic.
  • Meeting place (in Sophie's story): the canal.
  • Jansie's role: Sophie's friend and realistic foil.
  • Central ambiguity: whether Sophie actually met Danny Casey or invented the meeting.
"She saw herself in a boutique of her own in the High Street, not just selling things but going abroad for the clothes she sold."— narrative voice, on Sophie's fantasies

Poem · 01
My Mother at Sixty-six
by Kamala Das
Lyric PoetryFear of LossMother-Daughter BondAgeing & Death
About the Poet

Kamala Das (1934–2009), also known as Madhavikutty in Malayalam, was one of India's most celebrated and controversial poets writing in English. Her work is confessional, intimate, and boldly feminine — exploring love, loss, identity, and the body. "My Mother at Sixty-six" is one of her most anthologised poems, written with characteristic restraint and emotional precision.

Summary and Stanza-by-Stanza Analysis
Lines 1–5 (The Drive)

The speaker is driving to Cochin airport with her mother. She glances at her mother — who is sleeping — and notices her ashen face resembles a "corpse". She realises her mother is old and growing frail. The poem begins in the middle of an action, giving it an intimate, diary-like quality.

Lines 6–11 (The Familiar Pain)

The speaker looks away through the car window and sees young trees sprinting past and merry children playing. This contrast between youthful vitality outside and her mother's fading life inside is the poem's central visual metaphor. The speaker feels "that old familiar ache" — a childhood fear of losing her mother that she has carried for decades.

Lines 12–17 (The Airport Farewell)

At the airport, she smiles and waves goodbye. The smile is a mask — it hides her anguish. Her parting words are deliberately ordinary: "see you soon, Amma." The poem ends on these words, which simultaneously express love, denial of the fear, and the human need to maintain normalcy in the face of mortality.

Literary Devices
  • Simile — "her face ashen like that of a corpse" — the most striking comparison, linking the mother's appearance directly to death.
  • Contrast / juxtaposition — the mother's pallor vs. the "young trees sprinting" and "merry children." Life and death placed side by side.
  • Personification — trees "sprinting," children "spilling out" — they are full of the life the mother is losing.
  • Stream of consciousness — the poem moves between present observation, interjected memory, and emotional undercurrent without clear transitions.
  • Enjambment — the poem runs on continuously without full stops until the very end, enacting the unbroken, anxious flow of the speaker's thoughts.
  • Imagery — "late winter's moon" — cold, dim, peripheral. The moon at late winter suggests fading rather than full radiance.
  • Irony — the cheerful "smile and smile and smile" and "see you soon, Amma" mask the speaker's terror that she may not.
Exam-Critical Facts
  • Setting: driving from Pune to Cochin airport.
  • Mother's age: sixty-six.
  • Key simile: mother's face "ashen like that of a corpse."
  • Second simile: mother compared to "late winter's moon."
  • The "old familiar ache": the speaker's childhood fear of losing her mother — it has never gone away.
  • Parting words: "See you soon, Amma."
  • Tone: restrained grief, suppressed fear, quiet love.
  • The poem is written as a single unbroken sentence — stream of consciousness.
"And felt that old familiar ache, my childhood's fear."— Kamala Das

Q2Analyse the contrast between the speaker's mother and the images outside the car window.
Q3Why does the speaker smile and say "see you soon, Amma"? What emotion lies beneath this?

Poem · 02
An Elementary School Classroom in a Slum
by Stephen Spender
Social Protest PoetryPoverty & EducationClass Inequality
About the Poet

Stephen Spender (1909–1995) was a British poet and essayist associated with the 1930s generation of left-wing, socially engaged poets (alongside W.H. Auden and C. Day Lewis). His poetry is marked by compassion for the poor and a conviction that art has a social responsibility. This poem is a passionate plea for genuine education as liberation for slum children.

Stanza-by-Stanza Analysis
Stanza 1 — The Children

The children are described with stark, physical detail: pale faces like rootless weeds. Their eyes are recessed ("rat-grey"). One child has a "paper-seeming" bone-thin body. A sweet and young girl inherits her father's "disease." These descriptions emphasise poverty's bodily mark — it deforms and diminishes children before they can begin.

Stanza 2 — The Classroom

The classroom walls display maps and pictures of a world inaccessible to these children — Shakespeare, capes of hope, domed buildings, and cloudless skies. The contrast between what is on the walls (the world of privilege and beauty) and what lies outside the window (slag heaps, narrow lanes) is savage. The "civilised dome" of wealth rides over these children's lives.

Stanza 3 — Trapped Futures

The speaker observes that these maps and texts are a lie — the world they depict is not theirs. The children's "future is painted with fog." The world offers them nothing. The "map" on the wall becomes a symbol of exclusion — it charts a geography from which they are banned.

Stanza 4 — The Plea

The final stanza is a direct address to "governor, inspector, visitor" — those in power. Spender urges them to break open the windows, let the children out into fields and rivers, let them truly learn the world that was made for all. Education must be liberation, not decoration. Only then will history be "let out" — only then will the children's maps match the real world they deserve to inherit.

Literary Devices
  • Metaphor — children as "rootless weeds" — displaced, unwanted, growing without nourishment.
  • Contrast / irony — rich maps and paintings of beautiful places on the walls of a classroom that overlooks slag heaps.
  • Imagery — "fog," "slag heap," "narrow street sealed in with fog" — industrial bleakness as a condition of poverty.
  • Symbolism — the map and Shakespeare both symbolise a privileged cultural world from which slum children are excluded.
  • Direct address — the final stanza addresses the poem outward to the powerful, making it a social manifesto.
  • Alliteration — "future is painted with fog," "slums as big as doom" — bleak sonic textures.
Exam-Critical Facts
  • Children described as: "rootless weeds," "paper-seeming boy," "rat-grey".
  • Classroom decorations: Shakespeare, maps, donated pictures of a beautiful outside world.
  • Outside the window: slag heaps, narrow street, foggy sky.
  • Addressed to: "governor, inspector, visitor" — those with power to change education.
  • Key symbol: the map — of a world the children can see but not access.
  • The poem's demand: break open the windows — let the children's world match the real world.
  • Tone: protest, compassion, urgency.
"Unless, governor, inspector, visitor, / This map becomes their window and these windows / That shut upon their lives like catacombs, / Break O break open till they break the town."— Stephen Spender

Poem · 03
Keeping Quiet
by Pablo Neruda (translated from Spanish)
Philosophical PoetrySilence & IntrospectionPacifismNature & Renewal
About the Poet

Pablo Neruda (1904–1973) was a Chilean poet and diplomat who won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1971. His poetry ranges from passionate love lyrics to politically charged social verse. "Keeping Quiet" is a meditation on stillness — not as passivity, but as conscious, shared pause — and on the earth's capacity for self-renewal. It is both a pacifist and an ecological manifesto.

Stanza-by-Stanza Analysis
Stanza 1 — The Counting

The speaker asks everyone to count to twelve and keep still. The number twelve is significant — it echoes a clock's face, suggesting the suspension of time itself.

Stanza 2 — Stopping All Activity

He asks: for once, let no one speak a language. Let there be no movement of arms. A moment of total inaction. This is not laziness — it is deliberate, shared stillness. He envisions that if everyone stopped at the same moment, it would be a miraculous, unprecedented silence.

Stanza 3 — No War, No Engines

In this moment of quiet, wars would cease. Men who prepare chemical weapons would not know what to do with themselves; those who cut grass would have nowhere to advance. Even those who feel victorious or defeated would find those identities dissolved.

Stanza 4 — Not Death

Neruda explicitly clarifies: this is not death. He does not want permanent silence — that would be nihilism. He wants a living silence, not a dead one.

Stanza 5 — The Earth's Lesson

He draws the example of the earth, which seems to keep still in winter but is alive beneath — preparing roots, seeds, renewal. Life survives through apparent inactivity. The poem ends with a quiet urgency: perhaps if humanity paused, it too could prepare for something larger.

Themes
  • Introspection and self-understanding. Stillness allows individuals to know themselves. The noise of modern life prevents genuine self-reflection.
  • Pacifism. The poem imagines a world where weapons lie idle, aggression ceases, and human beings re-examine their capacity for destruction. It is a veiled anti-war statement.
  • Ecological consciousness. The earth's winter stillness as a metaphor for regeneration — nature is Neruda's model for how pause and silence can produce renewal.
  • Human brotherhood. In the moment of shared silence, all distinctions — victor, victim, aggressor, labourer — dissolve. Shared humanity re-emerges.
Literary Devices
  • Imagery — "fishermen in the cold sea would not harm whales"; the earth keeping still in winter — vivid, gentle images of suspended violence.
  • Paradox — "a huge silence might interrupt this sadness" — silence interrupting, rather than being interrupted, is counterintuitive and powerful.
  • Symbolism — counting to twelve (time suspended); the earth in winter (apparent death masking renewal).
  • Tone — contemplative, gently urgent, never preachy. The poet says "perhaps" and "maybe," not "must."
  • Anaphora — repeated constructions ("let's", "not", "I want") give the poem a meditative, incantatory rhythm.
Exam-Critical Facts
  • Count to: twelve (suggestion of a clock — suspending time).
  • Neruda's clarification: this is not death — silence must be living, not lifeless.
  • Earth metaphor: earth is alive in winter beneath apparent stillness — seeds and roots wait.
  • Nobel Prize in Literature: 1971.
  • The poem's central paradox: stillness as action; silence as communication.
  • Tone: philosophical, pacifist, ecological.
  • Original language: Spanish.
"What I want should not be confused with total inactivity. Life is what it is about."— Pablo Neruda, Keeping Quiet

Q2Why does the poet ask everyone to count to twelve? What is the significance of this number?
Q3How does the image of the earth in winter reinforce the poem's central message?
Q4What is the pacifist message of the poem? How does Neruda convey it without being preachy?
Q5Discuss the themes of introspection and ecological consciousness in "Keeping Quiet."

Poem · 04
A Thing of Beauty
by John Keats
Romantic PoetryBeauty as SolaceEndymionImmortality of Beauty
About the Poet

John Keats (1795–1821) was one of the greatest English Romantic poets. His work is characterised by sensuous imagery, a profound engagement with beauty and mortality, and what he called "negative capability" — the capacity to remain in uncertainty without irritably reaching after fact. "A Thing of Beauty" is the opening passage of Endymion (1818), a mythological narrative poem based on the Greek myth of Endymion, the shepherd beloved of Selene (the moon).

Line-by-Line Analysis
Lines 1–5 (The Opening Declaration)

"A thing of beauty is a joy forever." This immortal opening declares that beauty is not temporary — it creates a "bower of quietness" in the mind. Its loveliness increases over time; it never passes into nothingness. Beauty is an inexhaustible source of psychic sustenance.

Lines 6–13 (The Burden of Life)

The poet acknowledges human suffering — the "despondence," "the unhealthy and over-darkened ways," the "gloomy days" — and says that beautiful things are our shelter, our "sleep full of sweet dreams" that helps us endure.

Lines 14–22 (Sources of Beauty)

Keats lists sources of beauty: the sun, the moon, trees, daffodils, clear streams, the heath, the musk-rose — nature's unceasing gifts. He also includes the "grandeur of dooms" (noble deaths in battle) and the tales of old poets as sources of beauty that transcend time.

Lines 23–33 (The Immortal Draught)

All beautiful things are a "moving image of eternity" — they are earthly manifestations of divine, eternal beauty. The poet calls them an "endless fountain" of "immortal drink" poured to us from heaven. The poem ends in transcendence: beauty connects the mortal to the immortal.

Literary Devices
  • Metaphor — beauty as a "bower of quietness," "sleep full of sweet dreams," "immortal drink" — consistently materialized as shelter, nourishment, rest.
  • Imagery — richly sensuous: sun, moon, daffodils, musk-rose in bloom, clear rills. Keats piles beauty upon beauty.
  • Alliteration — "bower of bliss," "sweet dreams, and health, and quiet breathing" — musical, smooth sounds enact the beauty being described.
  • Personification — nature's elements are vitalised; trees "branchwards cast" shade specifically for us, as if on our behalf.
  • Intertextuality — references to Greek myth (Endymion) and "old poets" connect this poem to a lineage of human beauty-making.
Exam-Critical Facts
  • Opening line: "A thing of beauty is a joy forever."
  • Source poem: opening lines of Endymion (1818).
  • Objects of beauty mentioned: sun, moon, trees, daffodils, clear rills, musk-rose, old tales of heroic men.
  • Beauty described as: "endless fountain of immortal drink / Poured unto us from the heaven's brink."
  • What beauty does: removes the "pall from our dark spirits" — it lifts despondence.
  • Key idea: beauty is not just pleasure — it is a spiritual and moral necessity that keeps humanity alive.
  • Tone: meditative, reverential, Romantic.
"A thing of beauty is a joy forever: / Its loveliness increases; it will never / Pass into nothingness."— John Keats, Endymion

Poem · 05
A Roadside Stand
by Robert Frost
Social CritiqueRural PovertyModernisationIrony
About the Poet

Robert Frost (1874–1963) is one of America's most celebrated poets, known for poems set in rural New England that use plain speech and apparently simple observation to explore deep philosophical and social questions. "A Roadside Stand" combines Frost's characteristic pastoral setting with a biting commentary on the condescension of urban "progress" toward rural communities.

Summary and Analysis
The Stand

Rural people have set up a small roadside stall — selling berries, squash, or farm produce — hoping to intercept passing motorists. They have painted an unskilled sign offering "wild berries" and the stand's existence is a quiet, dignified appeal for a share of the city's money that "flows by" on the highway.

The Cars' Indifference

The cars whiz by. Some stop — but only to ask trivial directions, not to buy. Others complain that the roadside stand spoils the beauty of the landscape. These responses are an ironic embodiment of urban indifference to rural need: the poor are judged as aesthetically inconvenient.

The Dream of City Life

The rural folk have absorbed city images through movies and news — they dream of the "polished traffic" and comforts of city life. But these are hollow dreams deliberately manufactured by those who exploit rural labour without truly sharing city prosperity with them.

The Poet's Anguish

Frost confesses he feels such compassion for these people that he could cry — yet he must restrain his "heart-sore" feelings. He questions the nature of progress: those who claim to help the rural poor (the "pitiful kin") often "take them in tow" to a life in "villages the folk would not have chosen for themselves."

Themes
  • Exploitation and exclusion of the rural poor. The motorists and the system they represent benefit from rural labour but share nothing. Progress bypasses the countryside.
  • Irony of "help." Those who propose to help the poor — welfare planners, government schemes — often displace and disempower them rather than genuinely enriching their lives.
  • The aestheticisation of poverty. City dwellers who complain that the roadside stand spoils the view are revealed as morally blind — they see landscape, not people.
  • The poet's painful empathy. Frost himself is conflicted — he feels the injustice but is also part of the urban world that drives by. His "heart-sore" is a recognition of complicity.
Exam-Critical Facts
  • The stand sells: berries, squash, farm produce.
  • Motorists' reactions: drive past, complain the stand spoils the view, stop only to ask directions.
  • The rural people's dream: city comforts glimpsed through movies and news.
  • Frost's emotional state: "heart-sore" — compassion he must restrain.
  • Critique of "helpers": those who take rural people "in tow" to unsuitable city lives.
  • Key irony: the people who claim to want the beauty of the countryside are the ones indifferent to the people who live there.
  • Tone: sympathetic, ironic, quietly angry.
"It would not be fair to say for a dole of bread, / But for some of the money, the cash, whose flow supports / The flower of cities from sinking and withering faint."— Robert Frost, A Roadside Stand

Poem · 06
Aunt Jennifer's Tigers
by Adrienne Rich
Feminist PoetryOppression & FreedomArt as ResistancePatriarchy
About the Poet

Adrienne Rich (1929–2012) was one of America's most influential feminist poets and essayists. Her poetry charts the evolution from conventional, restrained verse to openly political, feminist writing. "Aunt Jennifer's Tigers" (1951) is an early poem that uses a quiet domestic image — a woman embroidering tigers — to explore the suppression of female identity within marriage and patriarchy.

Stanza-by-Stanza Analysis
Stanza 1 — The Tigers

Aunt Jennifer's embroidered tigers "prance across a screen" — vivid, fearless, unimpeded. They are "chivalric certainty" — they do not fear men. The word "chivalric" is significant: these tigers occupy the world of confident, dignified strength that Aunt Jennifer herself cannot access.

Stanza 2 — The Needle

Aunt Jennifer's fingers tremble with difficulty as she works. The needle is described as heavy. The "massive weight of Uncle's wedding band" sits on her hand — an image of the oppressive institution of marriage. The ring is not merely a symbol of love but of constraint and obligation that weighs her down.

Stanza 3 — After Death

Even when Aunt Jennifer dies, "her terrified hands will lie / Still ringed with ordeals she was mastered by." She will not be free even in death — the constraints of patriarchy will mark her forever. But her tigers will go on prancing. The art outlasts its creator; the tigers achieve what Aunt Jennifer could not: immortal, fearless freedom.

Themes
  • Patriarchal oppression. Aunt Jennifer is trapped in a marriage that diminishes her. The "Uncle's wedding band" is a symbol of the power men hold over women — not just physically but psychologically.
  • Art as resistance and escape. The tigers are the unconscious expression of all that Aunt Jennifer cannot be in life — powerful, fearless, free. Her embroidery is an act of silent resistance even she may not fully understand.
  • The gap between aspiration and reality. The tigers are everything she is not. This contrast — between the confident tigers and the trembling fingers that create them — is the poem's central tension.
  • Immortality of art. Even after Aunt Jennifer's death, her tigers prance on. The artwork outlives the artist's suffering, carrying within it her suppressed desires.
Literary Devices
  • Symbolism — tigers = freedom, fearlessness, power; wedding ring = patriarchal constraint, oppression; needle = the domestic sphere that confines Aunt Jennifer.
  • Contrast / juxtaposition — the prancing tigers vs. Aunt Jennifer's terrified, trembling hands. Art and life in direct opposition.
  • Imagery — "fingers fluttering through her wool," "massive weight of Uncle's wedding band," tigers "bright topaz denizens of a world of green" — each image layers meaning.
  • Alliteration — "prance proudly," "fingers fluttering" — both sonic and semantic: confidence vs. fragility.
  • Irony — Aunt Jennifer creates images of power she herself cannot embody. The creator is enslaved; the creation is free.
Exam-Critical Facts
  • Tigers' qualities: bright, fearless, chivalric, prancing.
  • Tigers' setting: "world of green" — vibrant, free.
  • Symbol of marriage: "Uncle's wedding band" — heavy, oppressive.
  • Aunt Jennifer's fingers: fluttering, trembling — contrast with tigers' confidence.
  • After death: hands will be "ringed with ordeals she was mastered by."
  • Tigers after death: "will go on prancing, proud and unafraid."
  • Poem written in: 1951 (Rich's early period).
  • Central tension: artist's oppression vs. artwork's freedom.
"When Aunt is dead, her terrified hands will lie / Still ringed with ordeals she was mastered by. / The tigers in the panel that she made / Will go on prancing, proud and unafraid."— Adrienne Rich
